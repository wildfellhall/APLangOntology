# Distinctive multimodal projects and analytic rubrics

Use this reference when a library's projects become interchangeable. A theme must change the intellectual task, not merely decorate an essay assignment with a new title.

## Design a consequential situation

Specify a role, an audience with a reason to care, a decision, and a constraint. Choose a form whose affordances make the concept visible: a dictionary entry draws boundaries; an archive records selection; a map distributes attention; a negotiated charter makes concessions consequential. Do not require every concept to produce the same three perspectives and generic poster.

Offer subject areas or open issues as topic suggestions, not completed artifacts or model theses. Preserve student choice of position and evidence. Explain exactly what must be submitted: number and length of texts, how they relate, what the visual must encode, what evidence is needed, and what a reader test should reveal. Vary the composition and test to suit the inquiry. Reusable analysis and reflection procedures can support consistency without replacing the distinctive task.

The visual must represent a relationship students could defend, such as a boundary, dependency, comparison, omission, or uncertainty. Ask for a linear text equivalent and assess the reasoning rather than expensive materials or drawing ability. Public posting, private interviews, purchases, and personal disclosure should not be necessary to complete an ordinary classroom inquiry.

Self-analysis should quote or precisely describe the student's own choices and explain mechanism, plausible audience effect, and purpose. A test should expose a genuine uncertainty: reconstruct a claim, classify a borderline case, follow a route, challenge an inference, or use a document without help. Require substantive before-and-after changes and a defense of the remaining position. Reflection needs evidence of decisions and a bounded transfer claim, not a report of enjoyment or effort.

## Make the rubric usable before work begins

Use an analytic rubric whose criteria match the deliverables. At least the central concept criterion and the visual reasoning criterion should state what success means for this particular project. Distinguish levels through observable differences in reasoning, evidence, accuracy, and execution; do not merely replace excellent with good and fair.

Give weights and a transparent conversion from levels to points, including missing work. Shared criteria may assess evidence, self-analysis, revision, and reflection, but should not erase the project's central challenge. A classroom project score is separate from the AP essay rubric and cannot predict an AP exam score. Evaluate the supported argument, not agreement with its position.

## Review

Can a student begin without asking what the deliverable means? Does each rubric criterion correspond to requested work? Could someone earn a high score with inexpensive materials? Does the topic list leave the thesis and project decisions open? If titles were removed, would the projects still ask students to do meaningfully different work?
