# Assignment Library source and design review

Checked October 30, 2026. Nine text studies, 90 original reading MCQs, 27 original essay prompts, three shared six-source synthesis packets, and nine projects. Historical selections are public domain in the United States. Modern website introductions are not reproduced. Editorial context, questions, fictional documents, datasets, diagrams, and projects are original.

## Assessment patterns

The [College Board Course and Exam Description](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-english-language-and-composition-course-and-exam-description.pdf), effective fall 2024, was consulted directly, including sample reading and revision items. The [exam overview](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-english-language-and-composition/exam) confirms the distinction between synthesis, rhetorical analysis, and argument. The [2026 task-specific scoring guidelines](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap26-sg-english-language.pdf) remain linked for full essay decision rules.

The new sets focus on reading: function of detail, structural development, audience appeal, scope of claims, qualification, and relationships among clauses. Four options and a rationale for each are supplied. Every item is grounded in the displayed text; historical trivia and outside legal knowledge are unnecessary. These are focused classroom sets, not a representation of the exam’s reading/revision distribution.

An editorial review shortened conspicuously fuller correct answers and replaced weak alternatives with errors about scope, voice, inference, or function. Twenty-six of the 90 intended answers are the longest option after that review; length is not a reliable key. Correct positions are distributed two or three times per set and shuffled deterministically, without a repeating four-item cycle. Answers use new `library-v1-*` IDs. Changing a published option order or key requires a version change or explicit migration so saved answers retain their meaning.

Rhetorical-analysis prompts provide author, occasion, audience context, and a sustained passage, then ask how choices contribute to purpose. Argument prompts invite a defensible, qualified position with relevant evidence. Synthesis prompts require at least three of six sources and an argument that connects them. The condensed 1/4/1 self-review is clearly distinguished from complete scoring rules and does not generate a score.

## Synthesis packets

Each theme has six fully available sources: three attributed primary selections, two explicitly fictional position documents, and a clearly simulated table with an original conceptual diagram. Nine prompts share these three packets; they are not advertised as nine independent source collections.

- Public memory: Douglass, Lincoln, and Thoreau; competing views of commemoration; a 40-volunteer trial with unequal, self-selected groups. No causal or representative claim is warranted.
- Membership: Declaration, Anthony, and Bill of Rights; delegation versus practical access; 60 fictional members classified into four mutually exclusive access groups. Either meeting or online participation reaches 48 under the stated assumptions, leaving 12 outside both channels.
- Education: Brown, Wollstonecraft, and Mill; resources versus opportunities for inquiry; two fictional classes of 24 and 18 students with different teachers and schedules and no baseline. Denominators and confounds are explicit.

The diagrams are actual rendered flows with ordered text equivalents. Arrows indicate proposed sequence or an audit’s questions, not proof of causation. Full source material, table values, limitations, and diagram descriptions are included in text exports.

## Project design

The projects use different forms: commission address, museum exhibit, membership hearing, investigative newspaper, charter game, opportunity audit, paired editorials, branching narrative, and education prospectus. Each supplies topic subjects, a decision-bearing audience, concrete lengths and artifacts, evidence requirements, visual reasoning, rhetorical self-analysis, an adversarial or audience test, two substantive revisions, a defended position, and reflection.

The [Purdue OWL visual-rhetoric overview](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/visual_rhetoric/visual_rhetoric/index.html) informed the requirement that layout convey an argument. [Carnegie Mellon’s rubric guidance](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/rubrics.html) informed explicit criteria and performance levels. Each 100-point rubric gives 30 points to a distinct central challenge, 20 to a specific visual task, and common evidence, analysis, and revision expectations. Accessible text formats and documented alternate readings substitute for costly materials or unavailable peer testers.

Completeness and UI tests do not establish psychometric validity. Independent teacher review and student attempts can further refine ambiguity and difficulty.
